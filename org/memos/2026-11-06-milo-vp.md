## 1. The core problem, as jobs-to-be-done
**The child (6–12).** Big feelings show up in the body first: tummy, fast heart, hot face, "I can't". The child usually does not have words for it, and the natural move is to avoid (don't go, don't try, ask a parent to do it). Avoidance brings relief now and makes the fear bigger next time. That is the core loop CBT for child anxiety targets [P1][P2].
- *Functional job:* get steady, then try the thing.
- *Emotional job:* feel brave and capable, not broken or "a worrier".
- *Social job:* be a kid who can join in.

**The parent (buyer).** Parents of worried kids often step in out of love: answering every "what if", doing the talking, letting the child skip. Researchers call this **family accommodation**. A meta-analysis found a moderate link between accommodation and child anxiety severity when parents report both [P5], and SPACE (a programme that coaches parents to reduce accommodation supportively) was noninferior to child CBT in an RCT [P3].
- *Functional job:* know what to say and do in the moment, and what not to do.
- *Emotional job:* stop feeling guilty or helpless; feel like a good parent.
- *Constraint:* little time, wary of screens, no therapist yet (or on a waitlist).

**The gap Milo fills.** Kids' calm apps teach breathing and play sleep stories. Few turn calm into **real-world brave practice**, and few coach the **parent's** side, even though both are where the evidence is strongest. Milo does not replace therapy. It is a practice ground, with a clear handoff to professionals.

## 2. Value proposition and unique value proposition
**Value proposition, one line:** *Milo helps kids practise calm and courage in real life, a few minutes a day, with their parent alongside.*
Milo gives a child a warm, non-judging friend to check in with, steady their body with, and then take one small brave step. The parent gets short, practical guidance on how to back the child up without taking over. Everything stays on the device, there are no streaks to keep and no chatbot to confide in, and a "grown-up help" path is always one tap away.

**Unique value proposition, one line:** *Milo turns calm into courage: a body-first brave-practice companion that child and parent do together, off-screen, with no streaks and no chat.*
Three things together set Milo apart. (1) **Body first:** it starts where kids feel it, with body clues and a breath, which is the OT and self-regulation entry point, before any thinking work. (2) **Calm is the warm-up, the brave ladder is the workout:** each calm session ends in one small real-world step on a ladder the child built with a parent. That follows the graded-exposure core of CBT [P1][P2]. (3) **The parent is a co-player, not a spectator:** a "support, don't rescue" coach is drawn from the SPACE model [P3][P4], framed as education, not treatment. Success is measured by what happens **off the screen**.

## 4. The psychology behind each step of the loop
| Step | Mechanism | Evidence | Strength | What it means for Milo |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. How are you? (feelings + body clues)** | *Affect labelling:* putting a feeling into words lowered amygdala response in adults [P12]. In preschoolers, stronger labelling went with better regulation, and on days kids labelled more they recovered faster (14-day diary, correlational) [P13]. *Emotional granularity* tends to fall from childhood into adolescence, and measurement is still unsettled [P14][P15]. *Interoception:* self-reported interoception problems are linked to anxiety, **but anxiety is also linked to more negative attention to body signals** [P27]. | Labelling: adult lab + child correlational | **Moderate** (labelling) / **Emerging** (granularity, interoception training) | Labelling is low-risk and worth doing. Body clues need care: frame them as curious "clues", never as danger signs, and never ask a child to scan for symptoms. |
| **2. Breathe together (4·2·6)** | Slow breathing raises heart-rate variability and shifts autonomic activity in healthy adults [P8]. In a 2023 adult RCT, 5 minutes a day of exhale-focused breathing improved mood more than mindfulness [P9]. **In children (9–13), a micro-randomised trial found no immediate average effect on negative affect or relaxation; relaxation was higher only on more-worried-than-usual days, and only against a passive control** [P7]. HRV biofeedback in youth shows benefit in a systematic review [P10][P11]. In CAMS, adding relaxation training had limited impact on the course of anxiety, unlike exposure and cognitive work [P2]. | Adult RCTs; one child micro-RCT (mixed) | **Moderate** (adults) / **Emerging** (children) | Keep breathing as the warm, physical door in and the tool for worried moments. Don't promise "calm in 60 seconds". The exact 4·2·6 ratio has no child-specific evidence; let the OT advisor set paces per age band. |
| **3. One care skill** | Regulation strategies from OT practice: sensory input, movement, water, rest. A 2025 review of sensory-based interventions found **strong** evidence for deep-pressure tactile input and for caregiver training, **moderate** evidence that alternative seating does **not** help, and **insufficient** evidence for environment changes [P25]. Zones of Regulation: an evidence synopsis says it "cannot be categorized as an evidence-based practice at this time" [P21]. Alert Program: improvements on parent ratings but not teacher ratings in a cluster trial [P23] and an ADHD pilot [P24]. | Mixed | **Moderate** (some sensory strategies, caregiver training) / **Emerging** (Zones, Alert) | Use plain, safe strategies the OT advisor approves. Borrow the *idea* of self-regulation check-ins, never a trademarked curriculum's names, colours or materials. |
| **4. Today's brave move → brave ladder** | **Exposure** is a core CBT ingredient. In CAMS (n=488, ages 7–17), introducing exposure tasks and cognitive restructuring sped up improvement; relaxation training had limited impact [P2]. Across 39 trials, CBT raised remission of the primary anxiety diagnosis vs waitlist (49.4% vs 17.8%, OR 5.45, moderate certainty) [P1]. **This is clinician-delivered CBT for diagnosed anxiety, not an app.** | Cochrane + large RCT | **Strong** (as a therapy ingredient) / **Untested** (app-delivered, unsupervised) | Make the brave move a graded ladder, repeated until it feels easier, co-built with a parent. Strict scope: everyday worries only (§6). |
| **(new) Parent coach** | SPACE coaches parents to reduce accommodation and respond with support and confidence; it was noninferior to child CBT (n=124, ages 7–14) and cut accommodation more [P3][P4]. Guided parent-delivered CBT (ages 5–12) was as effective as solution-focused therapy and likely cost-effective [P6]. Digital interventions with a human (professional, peer or **parent**) element showed better effectiveness and adherence [P31]. | RCTs + overview | **Strong** (parent-led therapy) / **Untested** (app psychoeducation) | Parent content is where Milo can add the most. Present it as education ("what many parents find helpful"), not as SPACE or therapy. |
| **5. Grow together (5 stages)** | Kind self-talk: in adolescents, self-compassion correlates strongly and inversely with distress (r = −0.55, 19 studies, ages 10–19; correlational) [P16]. Growth mindset: weak overall effects on achievement [P17]; small and not significant after bias correction in one meta-analysis [P18]; larger for targeted groups in another (mental health d = 0.32) [P19]; one single-session RCT in ages 12–15 cut depression over 9 months [P20]. | Mixed, mostly older ages | **Emerging** for 6–12 | Growth should mark **practice and courage** ("you kept trying"), not talent or logins. Don't sell mindset as an outcome. |

## 5. Product changes that follow from the evidence
Ordered by evidence strength × value. All copy and content need OT advisor / clinician review before any child sees them.

1. **Brave ladder (graded exposure, co-built).** The child picks one everyday goal ("order my own ice cream", "sleep with the door half closed"). With a parent, they break it into 4–6 rungs. Each rung: a "how scary?" rating before and after (3 faces for 6–8, 5 for 9–12), repeated until it feels easier, then climb. "Not yet" is always fine. **Scope:** a vetted goal library plus parent-entered goals filtered against the exclusion list (§6). *Why:* exposure is the strongest active ingredient [P1][P2].
2. **Parent "support, don't rescue" coach.** Weekly 2-minute cards: spot an accommodation, a one-line validating-plus-confident script ("This feels scary, and I know you can handle it"), and how to praise trying. A "when to get help" card links to professional routes. *Why:* [P3][P4][P5][P6]. **Not** branded SPACE, not called treatment.
3. **Body-clues check-in (interoception, curiosity-framed).** After the feelings face: "Where's the feeling?" (tummy, chest, head, hands, nowhere/not sure) plus "engine" speed (slow, just right, fast; our own wording, not trademarked terms). Milo responds with a matching tool. No symptom lists, no scoring. *Why:* labelling [P12][P13]; caution from [P27]; interoception training is feasibility-stage only [P26].
4. **Breathe as a tool, not a promise.** Keep the hero. Add "use it when worries feel big" and a pre-brave-rung breath. Offer two paces per age band (OT advisor to set) and a no-hold option. Remove any implied instant effect from copy. *Why:* [P7][P8][P9].
5. **Thinking skill for 9–12 only.** "Worry detective": what does the worry say, what's the evidence, what would you tell a friend? *Why:* cognitive restructuring contributed to change in CAMS [P2]. Kept out of 6–8, where it would be too abstract (OT advisor to confirm).
6. **Sensory toolbox (small, safe, OT-approved).** About 6 calming or alerting options a child can do alone safely (wall push, self-hug squeeze, cold water on hands, march in place, quiet corner). **No weighted products** recommended in-app; that needs professional assessment. *Why:* [P25]. Content set by the OT advisor.
7. **Routine anchors (co-occupation).** Milo shows up at two family moments the parent picks: after school (check-in + brave rung) and bedtime (breath + "one brave thing today"). This places practice inside routines the family already has, rather than adding screen time. A bedtime routine is a promising anchor for regulation and parent–child time, though the evidence is a conceptual model and mostly in young children [P28].
8. **Kind self-talk, not mindset slogans.** Milo models self-talk the child can borrow ("Tricky. I can try a small piece"). Stages celebrate effort and courage. *Why:* [P16]; mindset evidence mixed [P17]–[P20].
9. **Skill bridge (fade the app).** From stage 3: "Milo-free challenge": use your breath or a brave rung without opening Milo, then tell a grown-up. Off-screen use is the outcome we want (§7).

## 6. What NOT to do
- **No diagnosis, screening or risk scoring.** No "your child may have anxiety", no symptom checklists, no severity scores shown to child or parent. That is diagnosis, which is both a clinical act and a regulatory trigger [L1][L2][L4].
- **No symptom scoring presented as clinical.** The ladder's "how scary" faces are practice feedback for the child, never shown as a measure of the child's anxiety.
- **No exposure for trauma, and no exposure to real danger.** Ladder goals exclude: anything about past frightening or hurtful events, abuse, loss, medical procedures, self-harm, eating, unsafe people or places, and dangerous animals or heights. Trauma-focused CBT is an effective treatment for children with post-traumatic stress, but it is a manualised therapy delivered by trained clinicians [P33]. Adding a therapy dog to it did not improve outcomes [G5]. Milo routes, it does not treat.
- **No replacing therapy.** If worries stop a child from going to school, sleeping or eating, or last for weeks, Milo says "this is one for a professional" and shows routes. The parent coach says the same.
- **No AI chat**, no free-text confiding (Decision 1, 5 Nov; [G1]).
- **No streaks, guilt, decay or "Milo misses you".** Already locked.
- **No borrowing proprietary curricula** (Zones of Regulation, the Alert Program®, SPACE): no names, colour systems, materials or "based on" claims without a licence and permission.
- **No parent surveillance.** The parent sees ladder progress the child chooses to share, never the child's check-in history.

## 7. Measurement plan (non-clinical, privacy-safe)
Engagement does not equal benefit. In a systematic review of mental-health apps for under-18s, two small RCTs and a case study did not show an effect on intended outcomes [P29]. Pooled digital interventions for youth depression beat no intervention but not active controls, and engagement was low [P30]. So Milo measures **skill transfer**, not opens.

| Indicator | Where it lives | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Share of sessions with a finished breath **or** a named feeling (current key metric) | On-device counter | Practice, not opens |
| Brave rungs attempted / climbed per week | On-device | Exposure dose |
| Before→after "how scary" drop on repeated rungs | On-device, child-rated faces | Practice feedback that the rung got easier; **not** a symptom measure |
| "Used a Milo skill without the app this week" (yes/no) | Weekly 1-tap parent card | Transfer |
| "Times I backed my child up instead of stepping in" (0 / 1–2 / 3+) | Weekly parent card | Accommodation shift |
| Grown-up help taps | On-device count only, no content | Safety signal for the pilot review |

**Rules:** no free text; no symptom scales in the product; counters stay on the device unless a parent opts in to a pilot export, which is aggregated and has no child name, no dates of birth and no feeling history. Validated clinical measures belong only inside an ethics-reviewed study run with a clinician, never in the shipped app.

**Pilot success (judgement call, for test 1):** at least 6 of 10 families report "used a skill without the app" in week 2, and at least half the kids climb 2 rungs or more.

## 8. Where the evidence does NOT support claims
1. **No study tests Milo, or any app like it.** Everything here is borrowed from therapist-led or lab research.
2. **App-delivered exposure and parent coaching are untested.** SPACE, parent-guided CBT and child CBT were delivered with therapists [P1][P3][P6].
3. **Breathing in kids:** no immediate average benefit in the best naturalistic child trial [P7]. The 4·2·6 ratio has no child evidence.
4. **Relaxation alone** added little to anxiety change in CAMS [P2].
5. **Interoception training** for kids is at feasibility stage (n=9, no control) [P26], and more attention to the body is linked to anxiety [P27].
6. **Zones of Regulation and the Alert Program** are not established as evidence-based [P21][P23][P24].
7. **Sensory strategies:** mixed; the evidence for environment changes is insufficient [P25].
8. **Growth mindset:** weak or no overall effect on achievement [P17][P18]; mental-health effects mainly in targeted samples and adolescents [P19][P20].
9. **Self-compassion:** adolescent, correlational data [P16]; little direct evidence in ages 6–12.
10. **Emotional granularity:** development is non-linear and measurement is debated [P14][P15].
11. **Companion and pet effects:** animal-assisted evidence is mixed and the strongest trauma trial was negative [G5]; the "helper" mechanic has precedent [C6] but we found no outcome data for it.
12. **Digital mental health for kids in general:** insufficient evidence for under-18 apps [P29]; no advantage over active alternatives [P30]; human support improves effectiveness [P31].

**Therefore the only honest claims are about practice and support** (§9.4), never outcomes.

### 9.1 Wellness vs medical device
| Market | What the official text says | Read for Milo |
|---|---|---|
| **US: FDA General Wellness guidance, issued 6 Jan 2026** (supersedes Sept 2019) [L1] | A low-risk product intended only for general wellness is covered by FDA's compliance policy. Category 1 wellness claims include "relaxation or stress management", "self-esteem" and "sleep management". FDA lists "a claim that a product helps treat an anxiety disorder" as **not** a general-wellness claim. Category 2 allows some disease references ("may help living well with anxiety") only where healthy-lifestyle links are generally accepted. The guidance is non-binding. | Stay in Category 1: calm, stress, confidence, routines. **Avoid Category 2** for a children's product, even though it exists. Never name a disorder. |
| **EU: MDR + MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 (June 2025)** [L2] | "Wellness or fitness apps" do not qualify as medical device software. But MDCG lists, as an example of software that counts as a medical device, software "intended to alleviate certain eating disorder behaviours" through "personalized psycho-educational interactive" workshops. Rule 11: software that informs diagnostic or therapeutic decisions is class IIa or higher; other medical software is class I. The intended purpose is set by the label, instructions and promotional material. | A psycho-educational game is a medical device **if its intended purpose is to alleviate a condition**. Milo's purpose must stay "practise calm and courage" in every channel, including ads and store copy. |
| **Israel: Medical Equipment Law 5772-2012** (Sefer HaHukim 2357, 14 May 2012) [L3]; **AMAR** procedures [L5] | "Medical equipment" includes a device used for medical treatment, and computer software required to operate it. "Medical treatment" takes its meaning from the **Patient's Rights Law 1996**, which includes medical diagnosis, preventive treatment and **psychological treatment** [L4]. AMAR registration routes lean on approvals from recognised countries [L5]. | We found **no explicit wellness exemption** in the law text we read. Therefore anything that looks like psychological treatment or diagnosis is a risk in Israel too. **Lawyer to confirm** how AMAR treats standalone wellness apps. |

### 9.5 Before any child pilot (hard gates)
1. **Clinical sign-off:** a licensed child clinician (psychologist and/or the OT advisor within her scope) reviews every screen, ladder goal, the exclusion list and the parent coach copy, in writing.
2. **Verified crisis pack:** every number is checked by a professional per region and dated. Israel candidates still **unverified**: ERAN 1201 (24/7 per ERAN's own site, checked in v1 [S11]); 105, the national centre for protecting children online, which covers **online** harm, not a general crisis line [L9]; police 100 and MDA 101 (not yet checked). Nothing ships with placeholders.
3. **Parental consent:** a written, plain-language consent from the parent (with the child's own assent in kid language), covering what is stored, where, for how long, and how to delete it. If anything leaves the device, verifiable parental consent under the rules that apply [L6][L7][L8].
4. **Safeguarding protocol:** who reviews "grown-up help" taps, what the team does if a parent reports a concern, and an incident log.
5. **Lawyer review** of positioning, the disclaimer, the privacy notice and the consent form.
6. **Ethics:** if any outcome data is gathered beyond the product counters in §7, an ethics review comes first.
